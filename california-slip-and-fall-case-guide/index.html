The seven factors

What attorneys examine in a California slip & fall

These are not a score. They are the core questions a California slip-and-fall attorney would review before deciding whether to take a closer look.

Factor 1

Where the incident happened

Grocery store, retail store, big-box store, restaurant, hotel, apartment common area, commercial parking lot, workplace, public sidewalk or government property, private residence, your own home, or other property — each has different rules.

Factor 2

Who controlled the area

Store operators, landlords, tenants, maintenance vendors, contractors, and public entities may have different roles. Ownership alone is not always the only issue.

Factor 3

What dangerous condition caused it

Spills, recently mopped floors, grease, broken flooring, uneven surfaces, loose mats, clutter, falling merchandise, poor lighting, broken stairs or handrails, unsafe entrances, potholes, parking-lot defects, sidewalk defects, missing barriers.

Factor 4

Whether someone knew or should have known

Inspection routines, prior complaints, employee involvement, and how long the condition existed can all matter.

Factor 5

Whether the warning was adequate

A sign or cone matters only if it was visible, well placed, and gave a reasonable chance to avoid the danger.

Factor 6

Injury severity and medical documentation

Documented, significant injuries are generally more viable for contingency representation than minor soreness with no treatment.

Factor 7

Evidence

Photos, scene video, incident reports, witnesses, surveillance, receipts, clothing and footwear, medical records, cleaning and inspection logs, maintenance records, work orders, prior complaints, ownership or management information.

Module 1

Did the business know — or should it have known?

Many California slip-and-fall cases turn on whether the responsible party knew about the danger or should have known about it. Attorneys call this "notice."

Actual notice

Someone with responsibility actually knew about the danger — for example, an employee saw the spill, a manager was told, or a prior complaint had been made.

Constructive notice

No one admits to knowing, but the danger had existed long enough that a reasonable inspection or cleaning routine would have caught it.

Employee-created condition

A staff member created the danger (for example, mopped and walked away, knocked merchandise off a shelf, or left a hose across an aisle). Notice is generally not in dispute.

Inspection logs, cleaning logs, prior-complaint records, and surveillance can all be requested by an attorney to address this question.

Module 2 · Common misconception

Does “Open and Obvious” Automatically End a Case?

It is one of the most common things people are told to discourage them from looking into a claim. The careful answer:

  • A clearly visible condition may reduce or eliminate a property owner’s duty to warn.
  • It does not automatically mean there can never be responsibility.
  • Other questions can include whether the owner should have repaired or protected against the condition.
  • It can matter whether a person had a practical reason or necessity to encounter the condition.
  • Comparative fault and the full circumstances may also matter.
  • Only an attorney reviewing the facts can determine how the issue applies.

Slip & Fall Evidence Checklist

Some evidence — especially surveillance video — can disappear quickly. Print this checklist or save it on your phone.

What to preserve and document

  • Photograph the dangerous condition (close-up and wider view).
  • Photograph the surrounding area, entrances, exits, and lighting.
  • Photograph any warnings, cones, signs, or barriers actually in place.
  • Save the clothing and footwear you were wearing.
  • Request an incident report and keep a copy.
  • Write down the names of any employees and managers you spoke with.
  • Get the names and contact information of witnesses.
  • Save receipts or proof that you were present.
  • Write down the exact time and location.
  • Preserve all medical documents (ER, urgent care, imaging, follow-up).
  • Document missed work and lost wages.
  • Ask in writing that surveillance footage be preserved.
  • Avoid editing original photos or videos.
  • Avoid posting detailed statements about the incident publicly.
  • Speak with an attorney before signing any release or giving a recorded statement to insurance.

Location guide

Where the fall happened — and why it matters

Store, business, restaurant, hotel

Commercial properties often involve inspection records, cleaning logs, incident reports, surveillance, and commercial insurance.

Apartment or rental property

Landlord and property-management control of common areas, maintenance requests, prior notice, and tenant- vs. landlord-controlled spaces can all matter.

Commercial parking lot

Can involve the business, a property owner, a management company, or a maintenance vendor. Identifying who controlled the area is often the first step.

Public sidewalk or government property

This may involve a city, county, school district, transit agency, or another public entity. Special claim procedures and shorter deadlines may apply, so prompt review can be important.

Workplace

Workers’ compensation, a third-party property claim, or both may need to be considered depending on who controlled or created the dangerous condition.

Your own home

A traditional claim against a third-party property owner may be less likely when the incident occurred in a home you own and control. A contractor, product, landlord, or maintenance company could still matter depending on the facts.

Injury severity

Why injury severity and medical documentation matter

Significant, medically documented injuries are generally more viable for a contingency-fee claim. Documentation builds the foundation for both the legal and medical side of a case.

Examples of significant injuries

Ambulance or emergency-room treatment, hospitalization, surgery, fractures, broken hip, head injury, back or neck injury, knee, ankle, shoulder, wrist, or hand injury.

Examples of useful documentation

Imaging, specialist treatment, physical therapy, continuing limitations, lost work, medication history, and treating-provider records.

FAQ

Slip & fall questions California injury attorneys hear most

Is every slip and fall a legal case?

No. A fall by itself does not automatically create a case. An attorney generally looks at the property and who controlled it, the dangerous condition, whether the owner knew or should have known, what warnings or repairs were in place, the seriousness of the injury, the medical treatment, and the evidence available.

How serious does my injury need to be?

Cases handled on contingency are usually pursued when there is a meaningful, documented injury. A short period of soreness with no treatment is generally harder to pursue than a fracture, surgery, hospitalization, head injury, back or neck injury, or other documented serious injury.

What if there was a wet-floor sign?

A warning sign or cone does not automatically end a case. An attorney would look at how visible the warning was, whether it actually blocked or limited access to the danger, whether you had a practical reason to encounter the area, and whether the warning was a reasonable response to the condition.

What if the danger was open and obvious?

A clearly visible condition can reduce or eliminate a property owner’s duty to warn, but it does not automatically mean there is no responsibility. Other questions may include whether the owner should have repaired or protected against the condition and whether a person had a practical reason to encounter it. Only an attorney reviewing the facts can determine how the issue applies.

What if I did not see what caused me to fall?

It is still worth talking to an attorney. Surveillance video, witness statements, photos taken after the incident, incident reports, and inspection records can help identify the dangerous condition.

What if I did not make an incident report?

A missing incident report is not a complete bar to a claim. An attorney can still pursue records, witnesses, surveillance, medical documentation, and other evidence. The absence of a report is one factor among many.

What if I did not go to the doctor immediately?

Delays in treatment are common and do not automatically end a case. An attorney would look at when symptoms appeared, when you sought care, and what the records show. If you are still in pain, addressing it with an appropriate medical provider is important.

What if the store says the fall was my fault?

Defendants often raise comparative fault. California recognizes comparative responsibility, which can reduce but does not always eliminate a claim. The full circumstances need to be reviewed.

Can a landlord be responsible for a fall?

Landlords can be responsible for unsafe conditions in common areas they control or for known hazards they fail to address. Apartment and rental property cases often involve landlord, property management, and maintenance company records.

What if I fell in a parking lot?

Parking lots can involve the business, a property owner, a management company, or a maintenance vendor. Identifying who controlled the area is often a first step.

What if I fell on a public sidewalk?

This may involve a city, county, school district, transit agency, or another public entity. Special claim procedures and shorter deadlines may apply, so prompt review can be important.

What if surveillance footage is deleted?

Surveillance footage can be overwritten quickly — sometimes within days. Sending a preservation request as soon as possible can be important. An attorney can help with that.

What evidence should I save?

Photos of the hazard and the surrounding area, photos of injuries, the clothing and footwear from the incident, witness contact information, any incident report, receipts that show you were present, and all medical records. Avoid editing original photos or videos.
